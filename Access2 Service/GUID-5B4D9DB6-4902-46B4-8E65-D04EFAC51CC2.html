Wash Pump Backlash and Wash Buffer II Software Changes

Summary

This document describes the theory of operation for wash pump backlash and the changes introduced for use with Wash Buffer II.

Background - What is Backlash?

Whenever a belt is used to drive a device, a small amount of slack in the belt must be taken up before the belt begins to move the device. After this stretch is taken out of the belt, the motor turning the belt, the belt, and the device all move together in a 1-to-1 ratio. This stretch in the belt is referred to as backlash.

Backlash is important because:
  • The fluid pumps (wash pumps, precision pumps, and substrate pumps) use pistons to move fluid. Stepper motors that are connected to the pump pistons by a belt, move the pistons.
  • To achieve precise and accurate pick-up and delivery of fluids, a single step of motor movement must equal a single step of piston movement. Therefore, the backlash (stretch or slack) must be removed or corrected-for at the beginning of each piston movement. Additionally, because the pumps move up and down, the backlash must be corrected for each time the piston changes direction.

Correcting for Backlash

A valve is attached to each pump that allows fluid to be drawn in from the supply or pushed out to the destination. Before a pump can deliver any amount of fluid, the valve is turned to the supply location and the piston goes down and draws fluid up (more than needed for delivery). Then, instead of the valve immediately turning to send the fluid to its destination, it remains at the supply location. The pump piston reverses direction and pushes a small amount of fluid back to the supply, effectively removing or correcting for the backlash. After this backlash correction, the valve is turned to destination and the pump piston continues moving up, and precisely and accurately delivers fluid to the destination. The backlash is corrected for because any slack or stretch in the belt (backlash) has been taken up when it pushed that small amount back to the supply. Similarly, when the pump aspirates fluid, the suction is applied to the supply location first before the valve is turned to the destination location.

Calibrating the Backlash

How many steps of backlash to apply can be determined in two ways:
  • The first method is to apply more backlash steps than the design of the pump requires. Two hundred steps are used with this method most often.
  • The second method is to quantify the steps of backlash by performing a backlash calibration. The simplest way to do this is to use the home sensor for the pump piston. Starting with the piston in the home position and corrected for backlash to move down, the motor moves the piston down, away from the sensor a specified number of steps. The motor then reverses and moves the piston up until the home sensor is activated. Because of the change in direction, more steps are required to return the piston to the home sensor than are required to move away because the number of steps required to return includes the number of steps of backlash. The difference in the number of steps it took to get back to the sensor from the number initially moved away is the backlash calibration number.

An advantage of the second method is that it minimizes how many backlash steps are required and optimizes timing. The disadvantage of this method is that the calibration is only truly accurate for the moves the piston makes during the calibration routine itself. Across the entire travel of the piston, the backlash can differ from top to bottom. The Access and Access 2 wash pumps use this calibration method to determine how many backlash steps are required.

Access and Access 2 Backlash Adjustment

The Access and Access 2 systems use a backlash calibration for the wash pumps. Because the backlash correction on these systems influences air gap size, it is sometimes necessary to adjust (or offset) the backlash steps applied to all wash pump moves.

In the backlash calibration screen (Diagnostics, Mechanics, Primary Device wash pump):
  • When the wash pump is homed, the stepper motor moves the piston up until it triggers the home sensor, and then reverses (backlash corrected) by the number of steps of the saved offset. The saved offset is displayed.
  • When the pump is moved to position, the wash pump performs the backlash calibration and displays the calibration. Important note: On the Access 2, the calibration minus the offset is displayed.

History of Access Backlash

During the reaction vessel wash cycle, the wash pump must dispense wash buffer to the shower for the main pipettor and to each of the vessels in the wash wheel. To perform this process with a single pump, required the tightest possible timing constraints. Instead of performing the normal backlash correction by turning the valve to supply, the valve was left at each of the dispense probes during the backlash correction move. The result is a small air slug or air gap, called a backdraw, being pulled up into each of the three dispense probes during the backlash correction moves.

In the late 1990s, when the side-dispense probes were first introduced, there was concern about wash buffer dripping out of the dispense probes after the dispense to the reaction vessel. Therefore, an arbitrary 50 steps of backdraw were added to the backlash setting, making the air gaps even larger.

A few years later, the large air gaps were of concern because of bubbles they could cause in the dispensed wash buffer. These bubbles could potentially interfere with reaction vessel washing and cause erroneous results. It was recommended to reduce the backlash setting to a point where the top of the air bubble is visible in the tubing just above the dispense probe.

With the introduction of the Troponin assay and continuing to the present day, the recommendation is to reduce the backlash setting even further to a point where no large bubbles are visible in the reaction vessels after running the dispense volume checks for each dispense probe.

Changes Needed for Wash Buffer II

With the introduction of Wash Buffer II (WBII), the entire concept of wash pump backlash correction on the Access and Access 2 had to be revisited. A key difference between WBII and the current wash buffer (WB) is that the surface tension of WBII is much higher than the surface tension of WB. What this means is that WBII likes to adhere to itself and the sides of the RV.

Another difference between WBII and WB is that the backlash calibration causes a smaller step value with WBII than with WB. This difference is most likely because WBII has better lubricating properties than WB.

With our current backlash adjustment recommendation and the air gaps produced, there is a chance that WBII could be spit out of the dispense probes leaving a large droplet of wash buffer on the inside wall of the RV. These droplets can cause residual volume failures and erroneous RV washing results. Because of the arbitrary 50 steps of backdraw added, the backlash cannot be set low enough to counter the spitting problem with WBII. Additionally, setting the backlash lower than about 60 to 70 steps under the calibration value has been found to cause low dispense volumes.

Three software changes are being implemented for the release of Wash Buffer II:
  • Change 1: Concerning the wash pump backlash setting, the most obvious change is that there is no need, or option, to change the backlash setting. Each time the instrument is initialized, a backlash calibration occurs after the initialization sequence. Ten steps are subtracted from this calibration and that value becomes the new backlash setting until the system is initialized again. The purpose of subtracting the ten steps is to minimize the size of the air gaps, and at the same time keep the dispense volume consistent and within specification. A change that is not visible to the operator is that the arbitrary 50 steps of backdraw (from the file, sideprbb.csv) have been removed. An important point to remember is that the new settings for the wash pump backlash for the WB are different than the settings for WBII. Therefore, the instrument must be reinitialized whenever switching between wash buffers. Furthermore, a limit of a 190 steps offset (cal max of 200 minus 10 steps) has been added. When a pump exceeds this limit, a warning flag is issued and a message is generated in the event log to have the wash pump serviced soon. This condition is not a fatal flag and has not been demonstrated to have an immediate impact on results, though that potential does exist.

    Why: The relatively large air gaps created by the current wash pump backlash were causing Wash Buffer II to be spit out of the dispense probes leaving droplets and bubbles of wash buffer on the interior of the RV. The droplets could cause failures of residual volume and RV washing-type testing.

  • Change 2: The RV aspiration sequence has been modified in the new software. The new sequence is similar to the sequence used on the DxI. Historically on Access, when the aspirate probes dropped down stepwise into the wash wheel to evacuate the RVs, air could be sucked in at the end of each step as the probe was lowered. With the new sequence, the staging or stepping has changed to keep the aspirate probes below the surface of the buffer throughout the entire RV evacuation. In the new sequence, the tips of the aspirate probes do not go any deeper into the fluid than they do now. At the beginning of the RV evacuation, the tips of the aspirate probes sit at the surface of the liquid in the RVs, to aspirate any bubbles.

    Why: The current aspiration sequence was causing the loss of particles in certain assays during the RV evacuation process with WBII. Additionally, the current Access aspiration sequence was having more difficulties washing the RVs (LumWash, LumWashSon and LumInc52) compared to the DxI with WBII.

  • Change 3: The script for running residual volume has changed in the new software. The current script fills each of the RVs at dispense probe 1 and then moves each of the three filled RVs under the aspirate probes and evacuates the buffer. In the new script, each RV is filled at dispense probe 2 and mixed like an assay. After each RV is filled and mixed, the script runs the same as the current script. An important note to remember is that the residual volumes are lower with WBII and higher with WB.

    Why: Running the mixers during the residual volume script more consistent results with WBII than without. Additionally, running the mixers enhances the test by simulating how assays run on the instrument.
